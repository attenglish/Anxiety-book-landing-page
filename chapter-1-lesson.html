Chapter 1 · Foundations

Why English makes you anxious
(and why that's normal)

Your blank mind in a meeting isn't a sign your English is bad. It's your nervous system doing exactly what it evolved to do — just aimed at the wrong kind of danger. This lesson walks through why that happens, and gives you three techniques to work with it, starting now.

Ready Try the physiological sigh

One long inhale through the nose, one long exhale through the mouth. Three rounds is enough to feel a shift.

A familiar scene

Maria's story

Accountant, ten years' experience

Maria reads English reports every day with no problem, and writes emails to international colleagues without much trouble. But when her manager asks her to explain the quarterly results on a video call, her heart races and her mind goes blank. She knows these numbers perfectly in her own language — she just can't find the English for them.

"I know this material," she tells herself. "Why can't I just speak?"

What Maria doesn't understand is that her English isn't terrible at all. Her brain has simply identified "speaking English in front of others" as dangerous — and anxiety is just doing its job.

Part 1

Three types of language anxiety

Most people experience some combination of these. Knowing which affects you most helps you target your practice.

Communication apprehension

Fear of speaking or interacting in real time. The most common type — reading or writing might feel fine while live speech feels terrifying.

Fear of negative evaluation

Worry about what others think of your accent or mistakes — often much stronger than the judgement actually happening.

Test anxiety

Stress specific to formal evaluation: exams, interviews, presentations — even when casual conversation feels comfortable.

Part 3 · Technique 1

The physiological sigh

Research by Stanford neuroscientist Dr Andrew Huberman shows that a double inhale followed by a long exhale is one of the fastest ways to calm the nervous system in the moment. Use the ring above right before speaking English, when your heart starts racing, or after an anxious interaction to reset.

How to do it

  • 01 Take one long, deep inhale through your nose.
  • 02 Exhale slowly through your mouth, longer than the inhale.
Part 3 · Technique 2

The 5-4-3-2-1 grounding method

Anxiety lives in the future ("what if I fail?") or the past ("I messed up last time"). Your senses only work in the present, so naming what they notice interrupts the loop. Tap each box and jot down what you notice right now.
